Aikido / Budō

Awase (合わせ)

Actively tuning to your partner instead of forcing.

October 8, 2026

Polska Organizacja Aikido · 11 min read · Technique

Mat scenes in this text are my own illustrations. They describe no specific people or events. Language and history facts come with sources. Each school's interpretation carries its author's name.

Friday evening, near the end of warm-up. You stand in pairs and your partner grabs your wrist. Katate-dori, the simplest grip in aikido. He holds hard. You do the technique the way you have done it a hundred times. The shoulder climbs, the second hand pushes, the wrist stays where it was. Your partner smiles to himself. You hear only: once more.

On the third try I stop two steps away from you. I do not fix your hands. I say: stop fighting the grip. Take a step that carries you off the line, and take the contact with you. Nothing else.

You do it. A step to the side and slightly forward. His hand still holds your wrist, but suddenly it holds something moving in the same direction he was pushing. The grip softens without any wrestling from you. The technique comes out almost as a side effect. That is the moment the word awase names.

The grip that will not let go

Stay with this pair, because everything that follows can be checked against it. The first repetitions always look the same. The grip is information, and you treat it as an obstacle. You tense the forearm, pull the hips back, try to rip the hand free or shove through your partner. He feels resistance and adds his own. After half a minute you are both tired and the technique has not moved.

The correction sounds banal, which is why it is easy to dismiss. A step off the attack line. Not an escape, just a shift by a hand's width. You stay close, the contact stays, but your body is no longer where his push was aimed. For a short stretch you travel in the same direction he pushes. Only then do you take over the direction.

Two things separate this from simple yielding. First, your posture stays intact. You do not bend over the grip or collapse onto it. Second, a moment later you are the one leading. Your partner loses balance or structure not because you beat him, but because you entered his movement and took away his base.

An essay from the Iwama community calls this exactly what it is: merging into the attacker's movement and taking control. Its author warns against the English word blending. Blending suggests softly melting into the attack until the two of you form one mass. Awase is a dynamic act, and afterward it is clear who leads. That is the Iwama line's position, not a definition for all of aikido. I cite it because it describes our pair precisely. The grip softened, yet nobody melted into anyone. One of us led.

There is a second half to the correction that I only mention quietly. You stay half a step ahead, because you read direction instead of position. Copying movement always arrives a step late. You watch where your partner's hand is now and try to imitate it. Instead, watch where his push is going and place the body there. The difference is small and it decides everything.

Hoa Newens of Aikido Institute Davis arranges this into stages of study. First tai no awase, bodies tuning to bodies. Then ki no awase, energy tuning to energy, including techniques on the move. Then kokoro no awase, heart and intent tuning to heart. At the far end takemusu aiki, the spontaneous birth of technique. This is her teaching model from a 2023 essay, not a transmission from the Founder. I cite it because it orders what the mat teaches. In the first year you learn to stop getting in your own body's way. Later you learn to stop getting in your own head's way.

The same word on the weapons rack

Same pair, a week later, but with bokken in hand. I set an exercise where both of you cut the same cuts in the same rhythm. Step, cut, step, cut. At first glance it looks like a warm-up. After ten minutes the sweat on your back says otherwise. Let your rhythm slip by a fraction of a second and your sense of distance breaks down. You are either too close or his sword passes through a place you have already left.

Our line, Toyoda and Germanov, has its own paired exercises. Here I rely on the Iwama line's literature, not on the practice in our dojo, so details at Sesshinkan may look different.

In the Iwama line this exercise has its own name. Ken awase. Next to it stands jo awase, paired staff work, and the form called ki no musubi no tachi. Morihiro Saito, a direct student of the Founder, said it plainly in an interview for Aikido Journal in Rome in 1986. In aikido, everything starts with awase: taijutsu, jo, and ken. The forms are different but the feeling of blending is all the same. Saito learned directly from Ueshiba.

Saito also described the teaching order there. First suburi, solo cuts. Then awase, tuned exercises in pairs. Then kumitachi and kumijo, applied forms. The distinction is practical. In awase both partners often perform the same movements while studying distance, breath, and intent. In kumi the roles split into attack, defense, and counter. Iwama practitioner Tim Griffiths summed it up on the AikiWeb forum: awase is matched, kumi is paired. That terminology belongs to the Iwama school. In other styles the movements inside awase exercises may differ, so do not carry this split across all of aikido.

I like to tell students one more thing. The saying that good timing in these exercises takes about ten years is dojo lore, not a hard fact. My own view is simpler: timing grows from regular training, not from the calendar.

The word that shares a character with aikido

Only now do I reach for the dictionary, because we have already met the word on the mat. Awase is written 合わせ and comes from the verb awaseru: to fit, to join, to tune together. The Jisho dictionary lists meanings such as matching rhythm or tempo, joining, combining, and in a fighting context also crossing blades. The noun awase means a joining, a contact point, a fit.

The character 合 is the same character that opens the word aikido. The link is a language fact. It says nothing about technique, but it explains why many dojo take awase seriously. If ai means to join and to fit, then awase is that joining in action.

One more term is worth knowing. Awasegeiko means shared training in tuning. The Portsmouth dojo in the United States uses it in exactly this sense. Outside aikido the word awase has other meanings. In judo, awase-waza means a point awarded for two waza-ari. This article is about awase in aikido only.

The neighbors awase gets confused with

After training, names come up in the changing room that sound alike and mean different things. I sort them briefly, because each confusion costs something on the mat.

Musubi is the bond and continuity of the relation that forms. Awase is the tuning process that leads to it. Kuzushi is a possible result of good joining, taking the partner's balance or structure. In our pair the grip softened through awase, and the partner lost structure through kuzushi. These are not synonyms. They are cause and effect.

Ma-ai is the space and time in which awase happens. In short, ma-ai says where and when, awase says how. Mushin is a state of mind, awase a way of acting. A calm mind makes tuning easier, but it guarantees nothing. I treat this distinction as a teaching interpretation consistent with the sources, not doctrine.

Two loaded terms remain. Aiki and sente. The Iwama essay draws an equals sign and calls awase the principle of AIKI. Newens treats aiki as the principle and awase as its realization in the encounter. The model I propose here, that awase leads to musubi and musubi opens the possibility of aiki, fits her reading. I offer it as a working hypothesis, not a definition binding on everyone.

On sente the schools speak with different voices, and that has to stay open. Newens argues that sente belongs to the logic of competition, to the question of who goes first, and in an aikido without contests that question loses meaning past the early stage of training. Mike Swederska of Shin-Gane Aikido in St. Louis writes from the other side of the mat. In his view pure reacting fails against a fast opponent, since uke leads the attack. Nage has to seize initiative before uke commits fully, through gaze, contact, atemi, and kiai among other things. The two voices reconcile only on different levels. Newens talks about the strategy of the ego. Swederska talks about working with the mind under pressure. Hiding the difference would be dishonest.

A choreographed dance

Someone at the edge of the mat watches our pair and shakes his head. Cooperative training, arranged attacks, partners falling on their own. A choreographed dance. The Portsmouth sensei answers this charge directly, and he deserves a hearing. First, without cooperation there is no way to train subtle technique, because resistance from day one teaches only wrestling. Second, according to him, nonresistance belongs to the Founder's teaching. Quotes from Ueshiba in this article appear only as quoted via John Stevens' translations, through Portsmouth and Newens. I had no access to Stevens' books and did not check page numbers. This covers the lines about nonresistance, about having no fixed form, and about there being no contests in aikido. The accounts of the 1924 Mongolia and 1925 Ayabe incidents, which Newens cites from Kisshomaru Ueshiba, I treat as second-hand reports.

Portsmouth also writes that awase means tuning both to the world and to your own interior, and that there is no static perfection here because the world keeps changing. He wrote that during the pandemic, when training plans collapsed week by week. That is essay writing, not science, and I label it as such. Still, the idea earns its keep in one concrete place off the mat.

In a tense conversation, at work or at home, you usually do the same thing as in the first repetition of katate-dori. You hear the first sentence and start composing the reply. You interrupt halfway. You push yours through. Awase in conversation looks exactly like awase on the mat. You match the pace, listen to the end, travel alongside the other person with a question for a while before you lead on. My own illustration: you plan a trip with someone, the forecast falls apart, so you change the route instead of canceling everything or driving straight into the storm. I do not call this a fact about aikido. I call it carrying a habit, which some practitioners find natural and others find excessive.

Go back to the pair from the opening. Your partner grabs the wrist. You step off the line, keep the contact, your posture survives whole. The grip softens without wrestling. Anybody who trains awase knows this moment by a single sign. After the technique you stand straight and breathe normally. The rest is repetitions.
