Aikido / Budō

Heijōshin (平常心)

Ordinary practice is no ready-made answer.

October 19, 2026

Polska Organizacja Aikido · 5 min read · Practice

Contents
  1. Neither habit nor special state
  2. Practice continues in our relationships
  3. Sources and interpretation

Does “ordinary mind” mean simply doing what one usually does? If so, why practise attention and accept correction? And if not, does heijōshin become another image of an ideally calm self? That tension matters more than a promise that nothing will unsettle us on a difficult day.

In everyday Japanese, heijōshin means composure or presence of mind; dictionaries also record the Buddhist byōjōshin, “ordinary mind” or ordinary feelings and way of life.[1][2] Their shared written form does not make these usages interchangeable. Case 19 of Wumen’s Gate shows why the ordinary mind in this Chan dialogue cannot be reduced to a calm mood.

Neither habit nor special state

Zhaozhou asks Nanquan about the Path and hears: “The ordinary mind is the Path.” When he asks whether one can go toward it, Nanquan replies that going toward it takes one away. Zhaozhou does not stop there: he asks how to recognise the Path without trying. The reply rejects both knowing, which becomes false awareness, and not-knowing, which is oblivion. Wumen adds that even after awakening Zhaozhou still had thirty more years of study ahead.[3]

This is neither praise of autopilot nor advice to “stop trying”. It is not an instruction for manufacturing calm. Hershock describes Chan teaching as responsive to particular situations, rather than a set of universal formulas.[4] For a contemporary Aikido teacher, this can prompt a question: is the student responding to what is happening now, or merely repeating a familiar movement—or trying to look untouched? This is a modern teaching analogy, not evidence of a historical Zen connection to a particular training method.

Ordinary practice matters: it builds familiarity with form, ukemi and safe ways of working. But it is no ready-made answer to every situation. In a regular class, nage may notice that a partner is not ready, stop and check what is happening; uke may say that the contact or pace makes it unsafe to continue.

After a teacher’s correction, the student need neither defend the old version nor copy the new one mindlessly: try it, check the effect with the partner, and ask if the guidance is unclear. The teacher watches the choice and quality of contact, not a serene face. Either person can slow down or stop; an unagreed attack is not an exercise in attention.

Practice continues in our relationships

A similar tension arises at work when we find an error in information we have shared. We can focus on how we look, or check who has already acted on the mistake, name it clearly and correct the information. Composure does not erase consequences or responsibility; repair may require a conversation and accepting that the other person is angry or does not immediately trust us.

The same principle applies at home, but a family member is not an uke to be “guided”. If we answer a child unfairly or dismiss a partner, returning our attention begins with acknowledging what we did, listening to the response and repairing what we can. It does not mean demanding agreement or instant forgiveness. Composure is not obedience, suppressed disagreement or tolerating harm; sometimes a responsible response is to set a boundary, leave or seek help.

In both settings, preparation and habits help, but do not release us from noticing another person or checking the effects of our actions. This is a modest transfer of a question from the dojo, not a technique for controlling relationships, therapy or a guaranteed result. Practice may help us receive correction; it does not prove that someone is calm or right.

So return to the ordinary, not to an ideal image of yourself: a form we have learned is the beginning of action, not its automatic conclusion. A partner may change what needs to be done; another person’s response may show that our first judgement was wrong. Ordinary mind does not exempt us from further learning—Wumen leaves Zhaozhou thirty more years—and that is why the next movement must be checked in the world, not only in our image of ourselves.
