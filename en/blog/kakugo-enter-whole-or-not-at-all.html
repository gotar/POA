Aikido / Budō

Kakugo (覚悟)

Prepare for difficulty, then give the work your full attention.

October 12, 2026

Polska Organizacja Aikido · 9 min read · Practice

Picture a familiar partner exercise. You know the form, the instructor has set the pace, and your partner is ready. Yet you find another reason to wait. Adjust the grip. Check your feet. Start, then stop. Before you move, you want to be certain that this attempt will work.

Sometimes waiting is exactly right. Perhaps you do not understand the task, your shoulder hurts, or your partner needs a moment. Ask, change the conditions, or stop. But there is another kind of delay: you have done what you can to prepare, and now you are waiting for a guarantee of success. Training cannot offer one.

That is the moment I want to examine through kakugo: readiness for difficulty, and the resolve to act despite uncertainty. The aim is not to push ahead at any cost. It is to commit to a task you understand while staying attentive and responsible for your partner.

Readiness includes the consequences

Kakugo is written 覚悟 and read かくご. The Japanese dictionary Digital Daijisen defines its contemporary sense as mentally preparing to face anticipated danger, disadvantage or difficulty. Jisho lists mental preparedness, resolution and determination among its meanings. This is broader than an instruction to stop hesitating.[1][2]

Wanting a result is not the same as being ready for what it takes. You may want to pass a grading without being ready for months of preparation. You may want a difficult conversation, but only if the other person immediately agrees with you. Kakugo draws attention to what you are prepared to face when things do not go as you hoped.

The expression kakugo o kimeru (覚悟を決める) means to make up one's mind or prepare oneself for the worst. It does not establish that kakugo can only be a single act. The noun can describe readiness you already have; the expression describes resolving to act.[3]

The dictionaries also record a Buddhist sense of overcoming delusion and understanding truth, as well as resignation to something unavoidable. Nihon Kokugo Daijiten gives historical examples associated with understanding, remembering and mental preparation. Kakugo is not exclusively a samurai slogan or the name of an Aikido technique. Here I use its sense of readiness and resolve to explore training. This is a teaching interpretation, not a definition binding on every school.[1]

What are you preparing to face?

Return to the pair in the opening scene. You are learning ikkyo under an instructor's supervision. Uke, the partner who attacks and receives the technique, provides the agreed attack. As nage, the person performing the technique, your task is to practise the form you have been shown. You need not be certain of doing it perfectly. You do need to understand the exercise and know how you will respond if something goes wrong.

Readiness here can be quite ordinary. If I choose the wrong side, I will accept the correction. If my entry is late, I will not try to conceal it by pulling harder. If my partner can no longer receive the movement safely, I will stop. I am not promising myself a successful technique. I am committing to a way of working.

That commitment is not a decision that closes off further judgement. Once you move, you still attend to your partner, their balance, your own position and the space around you. You can adjust, slow down or stop. Accepting the possibility of a mistake does not remove the need to assess risk as you move. It can, however, help shift your attention from how you look to what is actually happening between you and your partner.

Tense shoulders or shallow breathing may suggest that someone is finding an exercise difficult. They are not a verdict on character. A beginner may pause because the movement is still unfamiliar. An experienced student may have a limitation you cannot see. A teacher's job is to recognise what help is needed, not to treat every hesitation as a failure of courage.

Full commitment is not maximum force

You can practise slowly, use little force and still be fully committed. You can also move aggressively without understanding either the task or your partner's abilities. Resolve and intensity are different things.

For this pair, the instructor sets a form and intensity appropriate for both partners. Uke should not unexpectedly increase the force of an attack to prove sincerity. Nage should not force a technique to completion to prove determination. Clear intent means working honestly within the agreed form, with room to slow down or stop. Our article on uke and nage explains that shared responsibility in more detail.

An unclear change of direction or a sudden pull can disrupt partner work. That does not make a harder or faster attack safer. Safety also depends on skill, fatigue, the conditions on the mat and ukemi: the ability to receive a technique and fall safely. Full commitment does not remove the need to account for these factors.

Saying “I don't understand yet”, asking for a slower pace or stopping because something hurts may be more responsible than proceeding without preparation. Kakugo does not give teachers permission to shame students or students permission to ignore a partner's signals. Readiness includes being willing to change the plan.

Hagakure: do not soften the history

Discussions of resolve in budō often turn to Hagakure. The work grew out of conversations between the retired samurai Yamamoto Tsunetomo and Tashirō Tsuramoto in 1709–1716. Columbia University's teaching material notes that it did not circulate widely beyond Saga domain during the Tokugawa period. Its later popularity does not make it a single code followed by all samurai.[4]

William Scott Wilson's translation includes the sentence “The Way of the Samurai is found in death.” The passage, from Chapter One, explicitly advocates choosing death when faced with a choice between life and death. It would be misleading to say that death is merely a metaphor for failure, or that the passage has only one legitimate meaning: freedom to act.[5]

This is a text rooted in historical service and loyalty to a lord. We can read it critically without adopting its ethics as instructions for a modern dojo. Our responsibility on the mat is to protect our partners' health, not to test their willingness to sacrifice their lives. I do not use this passage to define kakugo or prescribe technique.

Another passage is closer to the concern of this article: Part Two, section 17, on staying with the present moment. The version reproduced from Sources of Japanese Tradition connects that ability with years of practice and experience. This is not an Aikido instruction either. The modern application I propose is narrower: prepare before moving, then attend to your partner rather than your imagined performance.[4]

Resolve needs preparation

A decision does not create a skill. For a grading, preparation includes knowing the material, working with a partner, practising ukemi, training regularly and applying corrections. Greater determination cannot fill gaps in that preparation. Discuss readiness with a teacher who knows the student's everyday practice.

Resolve can help you begin the next task despite nerves. After a mistake, it can help you return to the work rather than try to rescue your image. It does not guarantee a pass or replace the requirements. It is one part of practice, not a shortcut around the others.

For this teaching purpose, I distinguish kakugo from concepts discussed in earlier articles. Gaman emphasises endurance through difficulty. Fudōshin concerns steadiness under pressure. Jiko sekinin directs attention to responsibility for choices and their consequences. Kakugo prompts a question here: am I prepared for the difficulty this decision may involve? These emphases complement one another. They are not exclusive categories or successive stages of initiation.

The decision you take home

Away from the dojo, consider admitting a mistake at work. You know what happened and what you can repair, but keep postponing the conversation in the hope of avoiding someone else's disappointment. Preparation is not about finding words that guarantee praise. It means gathering the facts, choosing an appropriate time and being ready to hear a response you would rather not hear.

Resolve might mean explaining plainly what you did, offering a remedy and not shifting the blame. If the facts are still unclear, investigate first. If the conversation becomes unsafe, you can leave and seek support. A delay can be sensible; the problem is pretending to prepare when you are actually avoiding action. This is an analogy with training, not a promise that Aikido can solve every conflict.

At your next practice, the useful question is not whether you can move harder. Do you understand the task? Is your partner ready? Do you know the limits of this repetition? If so, give it your full attention. If not, ask. And if the conditions change, respond.

Resolve does not replace technique or care for your partner. It helps you undertake work for which you are ready to take responsibility.

Sources and interpretation

Applying kakugo to practice and comparing it with other concepts are the author's interpretations, not claims about injury rates or the treatment of anxiety.
