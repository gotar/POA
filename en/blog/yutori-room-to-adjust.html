Aikido / Budō

Yutori (ゆとり)

Leave room to notice, respond and adjust.

October 17, 2026

Polska Organizacja Aikido · 7 min read · Practice

Contents

“Slow down” does not always mean performing exactly the same movement at a lower speed. Sometimes it means leaving a moment in which we can notice a mistake and do something about it. That can be the difference between finishing a repetition and learning from one.

Imagine a pair practising an agreed form of ikkyo. Nage, the person performing the technique, loses the required position and tries to compensate with faster hand movements. Uke, the partner who attacks and receives the technique, begins to hurry to keep up. Both want to complete the task. Neither has time left to see where it went off course.

The instructor stops the exercise. The pair agrees on a slower pace and returns to the position they were shown. On the next attempt, there is time to make a small adjustment before the final pin. They are still practising the same technique; the conditions for learning have changed. That room to adjust is what I want to explore through yutori.

Yutori: leeway and room to act

The Japanese dictionary Digital Daijisen defines ゆとり as having spare capacity rather than being cramped. Its examples concern finances and mental room. Nihon Kokugo Daijiten also explicitly includes mental, not merely physical, space.[1]

Jisho gives English equivalents including leeway, margin, reserve, elbowroom and time to spare. Elbowroom is a useful image: enough room to move. It is not a literal account of the Japanese word's components or its etymology. In this article, I use “margin” and “room to adjust”.[2]

You may also encounter the word in yutori kyōiku, a strand of Japanese education reform associated with reducing curriculum content and classroom hours. That is a separate context; here I am interested in the ordinary meaning: leeway and room to act.[3]

Where did this repetition run out of room?

Return to the pair. Telling them to relax is easy. It is less useful if they do not know what needs changing. Is the pace too high? Do both understand the sequence? Are they positioned so that their partner can receive the movement safely? Those are questions about an exercise, not judgements of character.

Time. A margin may mean working at a pace that lets both partners recognise the task and respond to a correction. It is not a blanket instruction to enter earlier. Earlier is not always better: timing depends on the form, distance and teacher's instructions. If every repetition needs rescuing through haste, revisit those agreements.

Space. The position must suit the exercise. Distance, called maai in budō, is not simply an empty gap between people. Too great a distance can be as unhelpful as too little. The pair needs room for the agreed movement and for ukemi: receiving the technique and falling safely. The aim is an appropriate distance, not the largest possible one.

Attention. Room to adjust means not being occupied solely with finishing the technique. You still notice your partner's movement and whether the agreed form remains possible. You do not force a plan that no longer fits. Reducing the number of elements in an exercise may help more than adding several new instructions at once.

Breathing and tension are worth noticing, but they are not a simple gauge of readiness. Raised shoulders may suggest difficulty; they do not prove laziness, cowardice or lack of skill. If you begin holding your breath and losing control during a faster sequence, tell the teacher and slow down or stop. Do not force deeper breathing to sustain an excessive pace.

The margin belongs to both partners

Aikido is partner practice. Nage feeling comfortable does not mean uke has equally suitable conditions. An experienced person may easily perform a movement their partner cannot yet receive safely. One person's spare capacity is not enough for good practice.

The instructor sets the form and intensity to suit both partners. Either person may slow down or stop. We do not test a partner's margin by unexpectedly speeding up or attacking harder. Our article on uke and nage explains that agreement in more detail.

A margin is not a safety guarantee. Skill, fatigue, health, surroundings and your partner's response still require attention. It may, however, create conditions where you do not have to fight the pace to make a correction. In the opening scene, a useful question is whether both people can pause, listen to an instruction and resume without pulling or rushing.

High standards can coexist with a measured pace

Slow practice can be demanding. You must maintain posture, attend to contact and avoid hiding mistakes behind momentum. Speed is not the only way to increase difficulty. A teacher can keep the pace measured while asking for more precise work on one element.

Conditions can change as training develops. The aim is not to remain forever with the easiest task, but to know why we are increasing difficulty and how to respond when it exceeds the pair's abilities. Doing less today may be appropriate when tired, returning after a break or rebuilding practice. Rest and recovery are not the opposite of yutori.

Discipline alone does not create every kind of reserve. Health, responsibilities and circumstances differ. Someone arriving after a difficult day does not need a lecture about managing themselves better. They need an honest conversation about how they can work today. A training plan can be adapted; it is not a measure of a person's worth.

Our article on kakugo distinguishes resolve from intensity. Yutori raises a different question: do the conditions leave room to carry out that decision responsibly? For this teaching purpose, the concepts complement one another. Resolve does not automatically create spare time, energy or space.

One delay in an ordinary day

Consider a day when every appointment ends just as the next one begins. The first conversation runs a few minutes late. Soon you are watching the clock instead of listening and trying to recover time by hurrying everything else. That is not proof of weak character. The plan may simply have left no room for something it could not predict.

If you control the schedule, leaving a gap or removing one task may change the quality of the rest of the day. If you do not have that freedom, the necessary conversation may concern workload, shared responsibilities or priorities. A margin is not always created by inner calm. Sometimes it requires changes no individual can make alone.

At your next practice, choose one element to examine with the teacher: pace, position or the number of things you are trying to do at once. See whether changing it lets both partners notice and respond to a correction. Yutori need not look impressive. It matters that there is still room for the next sensible movement.

Sources and interpretation

The partner scene and calendar example are the author's illustrations. Applying yutori to practice is a teaching proposal, not a definition for every Aikido school or a claim about injury rates. Dictionaries support the meanings of reserve and leeway; they do not establish the effectiveness of a particular exercise.

  1. Kotobank — ゆとり: Digital Daijisen (Shōgakukan) and Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten. Spare capacity and freedom from constraint, including mental room.
  2. Jisho — ゆとり: elbowroom, leeway, room, reserve, margin, allowance, latitude, time (to spare); JMdict data.
  3. Kotobank — ゆとり教育: Digital Daijisen and Nihon Daihyakka Zensho, context for Japanese educational reforms. Not evidence for judgements about students or recommendations for a dojo.
