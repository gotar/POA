Friday evening, partner practice. Walking between the pairs, I see the same mistake in opposite directions in two places. In one pair uke stands so close that nage has nowhere to step — the technique suffocates at birth, elbows go sideways, and after a moment both are shoving with their shoulders. In the other pair the distance is so large that uke needs two steps to reach, while nage waves his arms in the air pretending to lead something. I say the same sentence to both pairs: set the distance from which one cut lands and one withdrawal protects. The rest of the evening arranges itself.

That sentence is four hundred years old. Yagyū Munenori wrote it down in the Heihō Kadenshō in 1632: keep the opponent at a certain interval, above all remain calm, let him advance — and if you maintain the interval, he will not reach you. Japanese calls that interval ma-ai. Two characters: ma, an interval in time and space, and ai, fitting together. Not "distance in metres". A fitted interval.

## A fitted interval, not metres

In the dictionary, 間合い is a pause, an interval, a reach, a suitable moment — plus a separate fourth meaning: the distance between opponents in kendo. The second character, 合う, means to come together, to merge, to fit, to agree. So ma-ai is an interval that fits. Fits whom? This particular pair, in this particular moment, with this particular weapon.

The same pair, different days, different ma-ai. You are tired — your interval grows. Your partner picked up a jo instead of empty hands — your interval grows by a step. You face someone a head taller — your intervals are not symmetrical. This is the first thing to understand before measuring anything with a ruler: my ma-ai toward you is never equal to yours toward me, though the distance between us is one. Whoever dictates the distance dictates the fight. That is how the old koryu school puts it, and that is how it feels on the mat from the first month.

In the language the word lives off the mat. Ma-ai wo toru — to take the right distance. Ma-ai wo tumeru — to close in. The interval belongs to relations between people, never to dead things. The character 間 itself — a gate with the sun shining through its slit — sits inside the words for time (jikan), space (kūkan) and human being (ningen). A Japanese speaker hearing "ma-ai" hears not a ruler but a living relation. A scholarly article on the etymology puts it exactly: ma is not a void but an interval through which something will come.

## Three distances as old as the sword

Swordsmanship divided the interval into three long ago, and the division works on the mat to this day. Tōma — far, beyond threat. Issoku-ittō-no-maai — one step, one cut, about two metres, sword tips slightly overlapping. Chikama — close, contact. At issoku-ittō there is almost no margin: attention must remain constantly alert and unbroken. Kendo practitioners say outright that this is the point of a life-or-death situation — which is why almost every teacher tells you to strike before falling into chika-ma.

Yoshinkan, the hard school of precision, maps this onto the mat one to one. Tō-no-ma: no threat. Tsune-no-ma: the normal distance, about two metres, the length of one tatami mat — most techniques start from here. Chika-no-ma: contact, where you feel the partner's intent from movement, tension and resistance, and where kuzushi is born. Karate adds numbers: tōma is more than two arm-lengths, chūma one to two, chikama below one — plus a sentence worth hanging above the dojo door: whoever controls the distance controls the fight.

Donn Draeger, the man who measured budo with a historian's ruler, added the stakes: ma-ai is the distance from which warriors enter engagement, and the shorter it grows, the more courage and skill it demands. And Reverend Kensho Furuya of Los Angeles collected his teachers' definitions, and four came out, each true: the distance from which you see the opponent straight on while their feet and lowered hands stay in your peripheral vision. The angle from which you strike but cannot be struck. The spacing in which technique comes out at its best. Pick the one that works on your mat — all speak of the same thing.

## Weapons add a step

Take a jo and face a bokken. The stick is slightly longer than the sword, and that slight adds a whole step to the interval. Takemusu, the Iwama transmission line, says it outright: ma-ai depends on the situation — against a sword it is greater than against empty hands. The range of paired jo work corresponds to the far distance. That is why weapons are the best teachers of distance for empty hands: the stick does not lie. Stand too close — you get hit. Stand too far — you wave at the air. Hands lie for years; the stick corrects in minutes.

There is also a trap the old school warns about: habits from one weapon transfer to another. The hand "reaches" for the longer weapon; the thrust comes out too shallow after a system change. Anyone who trained jo after the naginata, or the short sword after the long, knows it from their own body. Distance must be recalibrated with every change of tool — just as with every change of partner.

## Distance and timing are one word

Ma-ai without timing is dead geometry. That is why the old school teaches three initiatives. Sen-sen-no-sen: you move first, reading the intent before the attack has formed. Sen-no-sen: you move exactly as the attack starts. Go-no-sen: you answer after the attack's full commitment — the attack enters your interval, and the interval becomes the counter's window. None of the three works at the wrong distance. Sen-sen-no-sen from too far is waving. Go-no-sen from too close is wrestling. Controlling spacing and timing are the keys to victory not only in swordsmanship but in aikido too — that is how the Los Angeles line puts it, and that is how it looks in every pair.

Entries are distance decisions as well. Irimi is entering straight into the technique — you take the interval, shortening it on your terms. Tenkan is a 180-degree pivot on the front foot, to the rear — you yield the interval in order to recover it at an angle. Kuzushi, the destruction of stability rather than a mere wobble, is born in chika-no-ma: where you feel the partner's point of lost balance. Whoever stands too far has no kuzushi — they have pantomime. Whoever stands too close has no kuzushi — they have wrestling.

And beginners? Instructors of every school have said the same for decades: they stand too close from unawareness of the attack, or too far from fear of it. Fear is key here, and I will return to it with the mind.

## The mental interval

Beside the physical one stands kokoro-no-maai — the mental interval. Momentary empty lapses of the opponent's attention, and the play of emptiness with fullness (kyō-jitsu), decide the advantage even when the physical distance is equal for both sides. Honestly added: this is the teaching of kendo and aikido didactics, not classical doctrine — the footnotes are thin. But anyone who stood in a pair with someone absent-minded knows what it is about. Their body is in the right place, and they are not there. The mental interval is open, and you walk in without knocking.

Hence the triad one teaching line proposes as a frame: de-ai, the moment of meeting — the present. Ma-ai, the distance to cross — the past, what brought you both here. Zanshin, the continuity of attention — the future. Signed: a didactic model, not a canon. But it orders experience. A technique after which attention dies has no future — and zanshin lists ma-ai among its very components, beside posture, gaze, shout and energy binding. That is how Charles Clark enumerates it, and that is how it feels: closing a technique is also rebuilding the interval.

Takuan Sōhō, the Zen monk who wrote to the swordsman Yagyū Munenori, did not write about distance. I state it outright because stretching is easy here: his Fudōchi Shinmyōroku is a treatise on the mind that does not stop at any point — not arrest but flow; not blankness but readiness in every direction. Mushin is not "thinking about nothing", whatever the internet repeats. And zanshin is not a menacing stare after the technique, whatever beginners perform. It is the calm vigilance of the whole body, which exposes no weakness. The mental and the physical distance meet exactly here: a mind that does not freeze holds the interval by itself.

## What I am not buying

Since so many stories circulate about distance, let me say plainly what I am not buying. Energy fields felt from three metres, letting you react before movement — no measurements; demonstrations work on a compliant partner and suggestion. No-touch throws as a teachable technique — recordings are demonstrations with a cooperating partner. Ki as measurable physical energy — Tohei, who first systematised the teaching of ki under Ueshiba himself, taught a model of mind-body coordination, not a field. And fixed metres — the interval depends on speed, weapon and angle, with the mental interval on top. Whoever sells you ma-ai in centimetres sells a ruler, not budo.

Plus the caution with numbers you know from the atemi piece: hard sources speak of seventy percent atemi in O-Sensei, not ninety. Ninety has no primary source; variants circulate from forty to ninety-nine. Likewise I will attribute no sentence about distance to the Founder — a literal O-Sensei quote defining ma-ai does not exist in hard sources. What I have, I sign with names: Yagyū, Draeger, Furuya, Saito through his students, Toyoda through method.

## How we teach it in Sesshinkan

I found no "which kyu gets which distance exercise" method in any line's papers — neither AAA's nor ours. What follows is therefore a POA proposal, worked out on the mat, not a quote from the transmission. I say it outright, because that is how we work here: verification instead of declaration.

The white belt gets one exercise to start. Stand with a partner. Extend "unbendable" arms, touch palm to palm or fingertips. That is your ma-ai — for today, in this pair. Lower your hands. Take a breath. Look not at the hands but at the centres: hara to hara, not palm to palm. And measure like this before every repetition until it becomes habit. The exercise comes from independent dojo — I cite it because it works — but the rule is ours: calibration before technique, not after.

The second ruler is weapons. Jo and bokken teach distance faster than empty hands because they do not forgive. Paired jo range corresponds to the far distance — whoever can hold the interval with the stick can hold it without one. I introduce weapons as a distance lesson before the white belt starts believing their hands measure well. They do not. Not yet.

Third is psychology, which Mangin wrote about and which I see every Friday: fear puts the student in the worst place. You back off to fist range — neither far enough to be safe nor close enough to work. The partner's full punch range, zero of your technique. The cure is the initiative progression: first go-no-sen, answer after the attack; then sen-no-sen, at the moment of its start; finally sen-sen-no-sen, before the intent forms. Each step is a different interval and a different calm. Do not skip steps.

And safety, because distance is also that. The most common accident on the mat is collision: one thrown onto another, falling into someone's path. The cures are prosaic: throws parallel in the same direction, a third person as "traffic cop", splitting into practicing and waiting halves, seated techniques when crowded, hands guarding the face in ukemi. Plus physiotherapist Russek's rule, which I adopt for us directly: teach from one third of speed and force, raise control first, speed later. Injuries come from mismatched speed and force or their sudden change — and the highest-risk group is sporadic trainees who spike volume before an exam. Distance also holds the calendar: regularity over bursts.

## Distance off the mat

Edward Hall, the anthropologist who founded proxemics, measured Americans' zones in the sixties: intimate up to half a metre, personal up to a metre and change, social up to under four metres, public beyond. I report with the caution owed to the reader: single-culture research from half a century ago, approximate, and Hall never wrote about aikido. But anyone who held a difficult conversation knows these zones from their own body. Someone stands too close — you feel pressure and step back or stiffen. Someone speaks from across the room — you feel the distance and stop listening. The interval exists in words too.

The protocol I take from the mat one to one. A hard conversation, a conflict, a negotiation: first the step — a pause, a breath, setting your own interval before you answer. The decision on the exhale, like the cut. Do not answer from fist range: neither in flight nor in clinch, only from the place where you hear and are heard. And hold zanshin after the conversation — attention does not die at the point of "it worked". Just as on the mat: closing is also rebuilding the interval.

## Closing

Masakatsu agatsu — true victory is victory over oneself. On the mat it starts earlier than you think: not in the technique, but in the place you start it from. Hold the interval that fits. Calibrate it before every repetition. Do not trust your hands; trust the stick. Stand neither in fist range with the body, nor the head, nor the word. And when you do not know where to stand — extend your arm, touch your partner's palm, lower your hands and take a breath. The distance that decides begins with that single gesture.
